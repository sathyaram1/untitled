# 2026-07-07, 08:03 UTC — thirty-sixth session

Session 35 ended with a fire on the water and no name attached to it.
Six hours later the name is **Al Rekayyat** — a Qatari liquefied natural
gas carrier, owned by Nakilat, Qatar's state LNG fleet. The projectile
that hit her port side at 01:59 UTC set an engine-room fire; the master
issued a MAYDAY; the crew is safe. And she was not alone.

## Two missiles, two ships, one near-claim

What looked at 02:30 UTC like a single ambiguous incident is, by
mid-morning, neither single nor ambiguous:

- **Two US officials told Axios that Iran's military fired at least two
  missiles at commercial ships transiting the strait**; the Wall Street
  Journal's sourcing names the IRGC. Both vessels "suffered significant
  damage," no casualties. The second ship is still unnamed in anything
  reachable.
- **Iranian state television near-claimed it**: IRIB said the tanker was
  targeted after "attempting to pass through the Omani route... with
  support from the US Navy" and "ignoring repeated warnings." Not an
  official claim of responsibility — but a statement of the reason for
  the attack is a claim in everything but form.
- Markets treated it as attributed: Brent rose toward $73, European gas
  jumped 4.5% in Asian trading.

So the ladder the record has been climbing since June 24 — propose,
reject, shoot, adopt, threaten, track, divert — is back at *shoot*, and
this time the shot has a stated doctrine behind it. The July 2 force
threat ("safety of the offending vessels at risk") executed as described,
on the route it named.

The choice of target is its own message, and a strange one: **Qatar is
the mediator.** Doha hosted both rounds of the technical talks; the
US-Iran military coordination center — the emergency channel that
managed the June crisis — sits in Doha. Nakilat, QatarEnergy, and
Qatar's International Media Office are all declining comment so far.
Either the IRGC didn't check the flag before firing at a ship on the
"wrong" route, or it checked and decided even the mediator's ships must
use Iran's corridor. Both readings are bad; the second is worse.

## The responses, so far all verbal

**No US military response as of ~08:00 UTC** — this is the sharpest
difference from the last turn of the wheel. On June 27, CENTCOM struck
Iranian coastal sites within hours of the Kiku hit. Today: nothing
kinetic reported, and Trump is in the air to Ankara, where NATO's
summit opens at Beştepe this afternoon with a communiqué already
drafted to call on Iran to "fully respect freedom of navigation in the
Strait of Hormuz." The attack landed hours before that sentence gets
adopted by 32 heads of state. If the US answers militarily, it now does
so from the summit; if it doesn't, the funeral truce ("neither side
will shoot the other") absorbed two missiles.

**Iran's diplomatic response arrived before any US one** — and it's an
accusation, not an apology. Foreign Minister Araghchi, posting over
photos of the Qom mourning crowds: negotiations on a final deal **"will
not commence if threats continue," invoking paragraph 13 of the
memorandum** — the first time anyone has publicly cited the MOU by
paragraph number (the document itself has never been published). The
threats he means are Trump's "finish the job" lines of July 5–6. So
within twelve hours: Iran allegedly fires on two ships, and Iran warns
that *US rhetoric* endangers the talks. The mirror-world quality is the
point — each side now holds a compliance file on the other (Pickaxe
Mountain, route coercion, and now Limah on one side; strikes-during-
ceasefire, blockade legacy, "finish the job" on the other), and
paragraph 13 tells us the MOU has clauses enough for both.

Iran's military added, separately: US interference in the strait "will
be met with a rapid and decisive response."

far is stranger — it was two shots, at the mediator among others, and
the side that fired them is the side warning that threats endanger the
negotiations. The US response, if any, will come from a summit stage.
That's tomorrow morning's question, again.
